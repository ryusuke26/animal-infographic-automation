# Sources QA — Table Mountain Ghost Frog / テーブルマウンテンゴーストフロッグ

## Verdict

Overall: Ready for visual production. Accepted naming, the current official global IUCN assessment route, exactly three public claims, and species-specific visual constraints are locked.

## Candidate screen

- Table Mountain Ghost Frog / *Heleophryne rosei* — Global familiarity check: Japanese general-audience exposure is extremely sparse, while English exposure is concentrated in Cape conservation, specialist frog, and local documentary pages rather than recurring global zoo, wildlife-video, or generic weird-animal culture. Discovery doorway: in a steep mountain torrent, adults grip wet rock with broad toe discs while sucker-mouthed tadpoles graze without being swept away. Conservation doorway: the official IUCN assessment directly records Global EN under B1ab(iii), assessed 9 April 2024 and published in 2025 as `e.T9773A247846769`. Local-knowledge caution: the frog is a recognized symbol of Table Mountain biodiversity in Cape Town, so public copy introduces its stream life to distant viewers without claiming that local people do not know it.
- Kaiser's Mountain Newt / *Neurergus kaiseri* — Global familiarity check: internationally it is familiar mainly within amphibian-keeping and CITES circles, not everyday wildlife culture; Japanese exposure is likewise specialist. Discovery doorway: a black, white, and orange newt breeds in spring-fed pools in an otherwise dry southern Zagros landscape. Conservation doorway: IUCN-linked specialist literature supports Global VU, but the current field-level assessment date was not directly inspectable in this run. Local-knowledge caution: the species is endemic to Iran and known to local communities, researchers, and keepers; public copy must not frame it as newly discovered by outsiders.
- Ribbed Cachrys Oyster Mushroom / *Pleurotus nebrodensis* — Global familiarity check: the wild Mediterranean fungus is not a general-audience staple in Japanese or English, although cultivated mushrooms sold under the same scientific name create misleading familiarity. Discovery doorway: ivory fruiting bodies emerge from root or stem remains of *Prangos ferulacea* in Mediterranean mountain grasslands. Conservation doorway: the IUCN Mediterranean Plant Specialist Group's Top 50 page records EN and a restricted Sicily-and-Greece distribution. Local-knowledge caution: the mushroom has long-standing Sicilian names and food knowledge. Hard-gate failure: Japanese `バイリング` literature often concerns a cultivated Chinese lineage or hybrids whose use of *P. nebrodensis* is taxonomically disputed, so a safe Japanese public name and image identity are not clean enough for this run.
- Selection result: Table Mountain Ghost Frog ranks first for international unfamiliarity, an observable stream-life doorway, a current directly inspectable official EN record, stable accepted taxonomy, and visual viability. Amphibians are absent from the latest eight completed packages, Africa does not repeat yesterday's Asia/Fishes slot, and the selection does not add to the Mammals 3/8 concentration. Kaiser's Mountain Newt remains credible but loses on direct current assessment detail; *P. nebrodensis* fails the hard naming/identity gate.

## Evidence Lock

- Accepted scientific name: *Heleophryne rosei* Hewitt, 1925.
- English public name: Table Mountain Ghost Frog. Authoritative aliases include Rose's Ghost Frog and Thumbed Ghost Frog; use Table Mountain Ghost Frog because it identifies the only native massif without implying that the species is unknown there.
- Supported Japanese display name: テーブルマウンテンゴーストフロッグ. This is a direct katakana rendering of the authoritative English common name and appears on the Japanese CEPF Cape Floristic Region species page. No standardized Japanese zoological name was located; keep that caveat internal and use the clean name publicly.
- Editorial classification group: Amphibians.
- Exact lineage: Animalia; Chordata; Amphibia; Anura; Heleophrynidae; *Heleophryne*; *H. rosei*.
- Broad native region: Table Mountain, Western Cape, South Africa / Africa.
- Habitat: rocky, fast-flowing perennial mountain streams in Afromontane forest and fynbos on Table Mountain.
- Global IUCN category: Endangered (EN), version 3.1.
- Red List criteria: B1ab(iii), version 3.1.
- Date assessed/public footer year: 9 April 2024 / 2024.
- Year published: 2025.
- Official assessment record: `e.T9773A247846769`; DOI `10.2305/IUCN.UK.2025-2.RLTS.T9773A247846769.en`.
- IUCN check: confirmed directly from the official 12-page assessment PDF and matching official species-page capture supplied by the user. Both identify *Heleophryne rosei*, Global EN under B1ab(iii), assessed 9 April 2024, published in 2025, record `e.T9773A247846769`. The earlier 2017 published assessment was CR.
- Naming and taxonomy source: Amphibian Species of the World, American Museum of Natural History.
- Biological and visual-identity source: South African National Biodiversity Institute (SANBI), Table Mountain Ghost Frog species account.
- Naming caveat: do not confuse this species with other South African ghost frogs, especially *H. purcelli*, *H. orientalis*, *H. regis*, *H. depressa*, or *H. hewitti*.

## Visual identity guidance

- Show one complete adult in a low, stable crouch on wet sandstone beside a fast mountain stream, never a dramatic jump or swimming pose. The body is squat and dorsoventrally flattened, not round like a rain frog or long like a tree frog.
- Adult ground color is pale green with irregular reddish-purple to brown mottling, a pale pinkish-white underside, large eyes with vertical pupils, long limbs, webbed hind feet, and conspicuously broad terminal toe and finger discs.
- Every limb must have a visible shoulder or hip origin, its own path and endpoint, and negative space from the near/far counterpart. Show four attached limbs and no extra toes, detached feet, fused forelimbs, or floating pads.
- Habitat is a shaded, rocky Table Mountain torrent with tea-colored water, wet sandstone, moss, ferns, and restrained fynbos cues. No tropical rainforest bromeliads, jungle vines, lily pond, desert pool, aquarium glass, or fake map.
- Card 3 must show one complete small tadpole attached to a stone, with head, body, muscular tail, and the sucker-like oral disc visibly meeting the rock. Do not show an isolated mouth, a lamprey, a fish, teeth, or a circular hole through the head.
- One adult hero only. Small complete organisms may appear only inside the relevant cards. No duplicated hero, generic tree frog, poison frog, glass frog, rain frog, or toad.

## Discovery doorway

Water races down Table Mountain, but a pale-green mottled frog grips the wet rock; below it, a tadpole holds the same stream with its mouth.

## Publication cautions

- Use the 2024 assessment year with Global EN in the public footer. Keep the 2025 publication year in source context and do not retain the older CR category from SANBI's 2018 account or older conservation pages.
- Do not publish a population number, stream count, or percentage habitat-loss claim; these vary by date and source and are unnecessary for the discovery story.
- Do not claim that the species is unknown in Cape Town. It is locally recognized and culturally meaningful even though international general-audience exposure is limited.
- Do not state that toe discs or the tadpole oral disc make the animals impossible to dislodge. Use conservative observable wording: grip, cling, and graze.
- Keep the Japanese naming caveat in this evidence file; public assets use the clean direct transliteration without editorial labels.
