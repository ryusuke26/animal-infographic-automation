## Evidence Lock

IUCN check: directly confirmed in the official DOI assessment record T15188A51140517; Global Endangered (EN), assessed 25 July 2015, published 2015, with a Needs updating annotation in the current record.

- Accepted taxon: *Okapia johnstoni* (P. L. Sclater, 1901); Animalia > Chordata > Mammalia > Artiodactyla > Giraffidae. English Okapi, Japanese オカピ. [IUCN formal record](https://doi.org/10.2305/IUCN.UK.2015-4.RLTS.T15188A51140517.en), [Mammal Diversity Database](https://www.mammaldiversity.org/taxon/1006343/), [Tokyo Zoological Park Society](https://www.tokyo-zoo.net/topics/news/ueno/8035_5701_2006-11-25.html).
- Naming route: the IUCN record lists *Equus johnstoni* as a synonym; the Mammal Diversity Database also records *Helladotherium tigrinum*, *Okapia erikssoni*, *Okapia liebrechtsi* and subsequent spelling *Ocapia johnstoni*. Search found no project collision under these names. “Forest giraffe” is an English descriptive alias, not a second taxon.
- Native habitat: lowland rainforest of eastern Democratic Republic of Congo. The IUCN record lists forest habitat; [Okapi Conservation Project](https://www.okapiconservation.org/the-okapi) specifies eastern DRC rainforest. No zoo enclosure or generic open savanna in the poster.
- Global status: IUCN record `T15188A51140517`, **Endangered (EN), A2abcd+4abcd, Global, Date assessed 25 July 2015, Year published 2015**. Current record has “Needs updating”; the footer reports assessment year rather than implying a new 2026 assessment. No population total or ranked threat claim.
- Claim 1: striped hindquarters and front legs can resemble a zebra, but the species is a giraffid — [San Diego Zoo Wildlife Explorers](https://sdzwildlifeexplorers.org/animals/okapi), [IUCN taxonomy](https://doi.org/10.2305/IUCN.UK.2015-4.RLTS.T15188A51140517.en).
- Claim 2: a long, dark, prehensile tongue gathers foliage — [Animal Diversity Web exact-taxon account](https://animaldiversity.org/accounts/Okapia_johnstoni/), [San Diego Zoo account](https://sdzwildlifeexplorers.org/animals/okapi) and [Okapi Conservation Project diet](https://www.okapiconservation.org/the-okapi). The prompt draws browsing leaves without forcing tongue-to-ear contact.
- Claim 3: the large ears can rotate independently, aiding forest listening — [San Diego Zoo Wildlife Explorers](https://sdzwildlifeexplorers.org/animals/okapi). No unverified hearing range or predator claim.
- Discovery doorway: the stripes invite a zebra comparison; the body belongs to the giraffe family. DRC communities have long known the animal; this copy describes a viewer's visual surprise only.

## Compact visual identity brief

- Individual/stage: one adult female; females lack male ossicones, so show no horns. Sex only constrains art, not public copy. [Animal Diversity Web](https://animaldiversity.org/accounts/Okapia_johnstoni/) documents the difference.
- Reference roles: [San Diego Zoo rump photograph](https://sdzwildlifeexplorers.org/sites/default/files/2017-07/okapi-02.jpg) for dark front torso versus banded hindquarters, side silhouette and legs; [San Diego Zoo tongue image](https://sdzwildlifeexplorers.org/sites/default/files/2017-07/unique_okapitongue.jpg) for tongue attachment and dark color; [San Diego Zoo ear image](https://sdzwildlifeexplorers.org/sites/default/files/2017-07/unique_okapiears.jpg) for large upright ears. The Okapi Conservation Project account grounds rainforest setting. Network sandbox blocked saving these reference JPGs locally, so the prompts name their source and visible geometry; no generated image is treated as biological evidence.
- Defining features: (1) narrow ungulate face with pale muzzle/cheeks and two large upright oval ears [Zoo head/ear photos]; (2) moderately elongated but non-giraffe-length neck attached to a deep chestnut/reddish-brown unstriped torso [Zoo side photo]; (3) high-contrast pale/dark narrow bands concentrated across rump and hind legs, with patterned front legs too [Zoo rump photo]; (4) four coherent slender hoofed legs with natural overlap [Zoo side photo]; (5) dark tongue extends from mouth to leaves, never from nose or neck [Zoo tongue photo].
- Natural pose: three-quarter side view browsing a low twig, allowing the striped rear to remain visible. A far-side leg and part of the tongue may be naturally occluded. Do not twist the body merely to display every limb.
- False silhouettes: no full-body zebra striping, giraffe-sized neck, deer antlers, or horse with a pasted striped tail. Keep a female without ossicones.
- Uncertainty: individual stripe geometry varies; do not lock exact band count. The card on ears illustrates directional rotation without prescribing an exact angle.
