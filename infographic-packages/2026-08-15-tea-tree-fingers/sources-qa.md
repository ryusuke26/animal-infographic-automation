# Sources QA — Tea-tree Fingers / ティーツリー・フィンガーズ

## Verdict

Overall: Ready for visual production. Accepted naming, current global IUCN assessment, exactly three public claims, discovery doorway, and visual identity are locked. The Japanese display-name caveat remains explicit.

## Candidate screen

### Tea-tree Fingers / *Hypocreopsis amplectens* — selected

- Global familiarity check: English and Japanese general-audience searches show specialist fungi, botanic-garden, and citizen-science coverage rather than household, zoo, aquarium, wildlife-video, or generic weird-animal familiarity; it passes the international-unfamiliarity gate.
- Discovery doorway: a cinnamon-brown fungus looks like blunt fingers clasping a twig, while growing over another fungus already living on the wood.
- Conservation doorway: the directly inspectable IUCN-derived assessment record confirms Global CR under C2a(i), assessed 26 July 2019; the errata version is record `e.T80188449A185681031`.
- Local-knowledge caution: Australian and New Zealand mycologists, Fungimap volunteers, and local naturalists already know and monitor Tea-tree Fingers, so public copy frames it as the viewer's discovery and never claims nobody knows it.

### Araripe Manakin / アラリペマイコドリ / *Chiroxiphia bokermanni* — not locked

- Global familiarity check: it is little known outside birding and conservation audiences and passes the international-unfamiliarity gate.
- Discovery doorway: a small white male with a forward red crest lives around spring-fed humid forest enclaves on Brazil's otherwise dry Araripe Plateau.
- Conservation doorway: the formal 2018 IUCN record remains Global CR, but BirdLife's 2026 official review recommends EN and states that final IUCN publication is still pending; that transition creates avoidable status timing risk for today's public footer.
- Local-knowledge caution: it is locally known as `soldadinho-do-araripe` and tied to spring-water landscapes and conservation work in Ceará; an outside-facing post must not call it universally unknown.

### Purple Frog / むらさきガエル / *Nasikabatrachus sahyadrensis* — rejected

- Global familiarity check: its pig-like snout and subterranean life recur in English and Japanese unusual-animal coverage; it fails the mission's global-familiarity gate.
- Discovery doorway: a Western Ghats frog spends most of the year underground and emerges around monsoon rains.
- Conservation doorway: an official IUCN species sheet exists, but the topic is already a completed legacy package and cannot be selected again.
- Local-knowledge caution: the frog has established local names and cultural knowledge in Kerala; a science-discovery framing must not erase that knowledge.

## Selection decision

Tea-tree Fingers wins on international unfamiliarity, discovery strength, directly supportable Global CR context, naming transparency, and visual viability. Fungi and lichens is absent from the latest eight completed packages, but that rotation value is only the final tie-breaker. The selected topic does not repeat yesterday's Africa/Mammals combination and avoids the Araripe Manakin's live 2026 status transition. The English common name and accepted scientific name are authoritative. No established standard Japanese common name was found; `ティーツリー・フィンガーズ` is used only as a transparent katakana rendering of `Tea-tree Fingers`, with the caveat repeated in README and both source notes.

## Evidence Lock

- Accepted scientific name: *Hypocreopsis amplectens* T.W. May & P.R. Johnst.
- English common name: Tea-tree Fingers.
- Japanese display name: ティーツリー・フィンガーズ, a transparent transliteration of the documented English common name; not claimed as an established standard Japanese name.
- Editorial classification group: Fungi and lichens.
- Exact lineage: Fungi; Ascomycota; Sordariomycetes; Hypocreales; Hypocreaceae; *Hypocreopsis*; *H. amplectens*.
- Broad native region: southeastern Australia and New Zealand's South Island / Oceania.
- Habitat: long-unburnt moist coastal or near-coastal woodland and shrub understorey, on fine dead wood of tea-tree and other woody hosts.
- Naming caveats: Biota of New Zealand marks *Hypocreopsis amplectens* as the current and preferred name. The Japanese display name is an explanatory transliteration, not an asserted vernacular standard.
- Global IUCN category: Critically Endangered (CR), criterion C2a(i), assessed 26 July 2019. The current errata record is `e.T80188449A185681031`; the original 2019 assessment identifier appears in older references, but the public footer correctly uses the assessment year 2019.
- IUCN check: Direct IUCN URL `https://www.iucnredlist.org/species/80188449/185681031` and the inspectable IUCN-derived assessment record agree on Global CR, C2a(i), assessment date 26 July 2019, and errata citation `e.T80188449A185681031`; checked 2026-08-15.

## Visual identity guidance

- One dominant macro-scale fruiting-body cluster remains attached to one complete thin dead twig.
- A low irregular orange-brown to cinnamon central stroma sends out several thick, blunt, flattened radial lobes that lie against and clasp the wood; paler tips are acceptable.
- The fungus has no cap, stem, gills, pores, animal digits, nails, roots, tentacles, or coral-like upright branching.
- Habitat cues are restrained tea-tree foliage, fine fallen wood, bark texture, and damp long-unburnt woodland light; no fire spectacle, mine scene, map, or flag.
- Card 2 may show the layered fungus-on-fungus relationship only as a restrained attached twig cutaway, with the whole mini-organism and surrounding wood intact.
- Do not infer that the visible lobes themselves are individual organisms or roots.

## Discovery doorway

A small cinnamon-brown mass wraps blunt fingers around a twig. Under it is another fungus already working through the wood: a fungus-on-fungus life hidden in the wet understorey.

## Publication cautions

- Do not call `ティーツリー・フィンガーズ` a formally established Japanese common name; it is a transparent display transliteration.
- Do not publish a population number, trend figure, site count, or extinction timeline.
- Do not claim a proven single host-fungus species; keep `another wood-decay fungus`.
- Do not turn its local survey and conservation history into a claim that local people do not know it.
