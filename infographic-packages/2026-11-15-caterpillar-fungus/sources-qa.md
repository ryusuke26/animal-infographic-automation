- Familiarity and discovery: 冬虫夏草 is a familiar name in Japan and East Asia as a traditional medicine; the less familiar supported aspect is the life cycle — the host caterpillar lives years underground, the fungus fills its body and leaves the skin intact, and the stalk grows from the head (IUCN account; National Geographic).
- Contribution to variety: first insect-parasitic fungus and first Tibetan-Plateau setting; a soil cutaway composition instead of a basking reptile; colour-lithograph wall chart medium after gouache and engraving.
- Conservation context: Global VU (assessed 2019), mainly due to intensive harvesting. The poster states only the category; no population number or threat claim on the poster.
- Local-knowledge caution: the fungus is well known to Tibetan, Nepali, Bhutanese, Indian and Chinese harvesters and markets; copy introduces its life cycle and makes no "unknown" claim. Medicinal-use claims are deliberately excluded.
- Selection rationale: a strong, visually explainable discovery with a directly confirmed official assessment date, filling the missing Fungi group with a way of life the series has not shown.

## Evidence Lock

- Exact taxon and names: *Ophiocordyceps sinensis* (Berk.) G.H. Sung, J.M. Sung, Hywel-Jones & Spatafora (syn. *Cordyceps sinensis*). English: Caterpillar Fungus (IUCN common name: Chinese Caterpillar Fungus). Japanese: シネンシストウチュウカソウ (used in Japanese scientific literature for this species; 冬虫夏草 is also a broad name for many insect-parasitic fungi, so the poster title uses the species-specific name and the post explains 冬虫夏草).
- Lineage: Fungi > Ascomycota > Sordariomycetes > Hypocreales > Ophiocordycipitaceae > *Ophiocordyceps* > *O. sinensis* (IUCN taxonomy).
- IUCN check: official record https://www.iucnredlist.org/species/58514773/179197748 read directly 2026-11-15 — Global Vulnerable A2bcd+3bcd+4bcd, ver 3.1, Date Assessed 31 October 2019, Year Published 2020, citation Yang, Z.-L. 2020 (amended version of 2020 assessment), e.T58514773A179197748. Range: Bhutan; China (Gansu, Qinghai, Sichuan, Tibet, Yunnan); India (Himachal Pradesh, Sikkim, Uttaranchal); Nepal; 3,000–5,000 m. Footer uses the assessment year 2019.
- Claim 1 (card 1): stroma emerges in spring or early summer (IUCN habitat/ecology); it sprouts from the caterpillar's head (National Geographic, Olsvik 2024); host is a ghost moth (*Thitarodes*) larva that dies underground, usually in winter (IUCN).
- Claim 2 (card 2): the host body is used as substratum for mycelium and converted into a sclerotium, leaving the exoskeleton intact (IUCN). "硬い塊 / hard mass" = sclerotium.
- Claim 3 (card 3): host larvae live underground for the entire larval stage of three to four years or longer, feeding on roots and caudexes of alpine plants (IUCN); prefer roots of grasses and herbaceous plants (IUCN).
- Main post (beyond cards): Tibetan name yartsa gunbu means "winter worm, summer grass" (National Geographic); the kanji 冬虫夏草 carry the same meaning; 3,000–5,000 m range (IUCN).
- Story reply (beyond cards): "indications in some areas that the caterpillar is infected soon after hatching and the fungus may stay dormant within its body for several years until the caterpillar is ready to pupate" (IUCN — hedged in copy as らしい / 見方がある / may / seems); northern India harvest in March–April as the snow starts melting, staying in alpine meadows for nearly three months (IUCN Use and Trade, citing Negi et al. 2006).
- Not used: National Geographic's "takes over the nervous system / digs upward" (hypothesis), stalk lengths in cm (sources vary), medicinal claims, prices.

## Visual identity brief

- Individual/stage: one mature fruiting specimen (dead larva + stroma) in situ; card 3 shows a living uninfected-looking host larva with no species claim beyond ghost moth larva.
- Biological identity references (saved in references/, Wikimedia Commons):
  1. `Freshly_unearthed_Cordyceps_sinensis.jpg` — Fumikas Sagisavas, CC0: freshly dug specimens; stalk colour (brown, granular upper part, darker pointed tip), stalk ≥ caterpillar length, soil-covered body.
  2. `CordycepsSinensis.jpg` — L. Shyamal, CC BY-SA 3.0, on *Thitarodes* caterpillars: single dark stalk joined to the head; ringed golden body with stubby prolegs.
  3. `Api-yarcha.jpg` — Deepak Raj Bhatt, CC BY-SA 4.0, Nepal: many dried specimens; consistent golden ringed bodies, one stalk per head (one forked stalk seen — natural variation; poster uses the usual single stalk).
- Diagnostic invariants: (1) plump golden-yellow to yellow-brown caterpillar with fine ring segments and rows of stubby prolegs (refs 2, 3); (2) exactly ONE slender unbranched stalk joined to the top of the head (refs 2, 3); (3) stalk dark brown to blackish, ≥ body length, slightly thicker granular upper part, short pointed tip (ref 1); (4) no cap/gills — a rod, not a mushroom (refs 1–3); (5) in life the body is buried head-up with only the stalk tip above short alpine grass (National Geographic description; IUCN habitat).
- Natural pose: near-vertical buried larva, head up; legs on the far side naturally hidden.
- False silhouettes: *Cordyceps militaris* (orange club on pupa); capped mushrooms; plant sprouts/roots; worm/centipede body.
- Card 2 interior colour (creamy white) follows the white sclerotium visible in broken dried specimens; the illustration is schematic.
