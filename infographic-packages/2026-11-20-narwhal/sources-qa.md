- Familiarity and discovery: the narwhal is widely recognized, and well known to Inuit communities who hunt it; the less familiar aspect for a general audience is that the "horn" is the male's left upper tooth, and that it dives beyond 800 m many times a day under nearly closed winter ice.
- Contribution to variety: first cetacean and first marine mammal in the series; first Arctic/polar sea setting in the latest eight; Mammals absent from the latest eight; woodblock print medium after collage, lithograph, gouache and engraving.

- Local-knowledge caution: copy uses viewer-relative discovery and makes no "unknown" claim; the unicorn hook describes medieval European trade, not Arctic knowledge.
- Selection rationale: a strong, explainable anatomy-plus-behaviour story with a directly confirmed official assessment date, filling the missing Mammals group in a new region and body form.

## Evidence Lock

- Exact taxon and names: *Monodon monoceros* Linnaeus, 1758 (IUCN, NOAA). English: Narwhal. Japanese: イッカク (一角), the standard Japanese name.
- Lineage: Animalia > Chordata > Mammalia > Artiodactyla (Cetacea; IUCN uses Artiodactyla, NOAA lists Cetacea) > Monodontidae > *Monodon*.
- IUCN check: official record https://www.iucnredlist.org/species/13704/50367651 read directly 2026-11-20 — Global Least Concern, ver 3.1, Date Assessed 03 July 2017, Year Published 2017, citation Lowry, Laidre & Reeves 2017, e.T13704A50367651; trend Unknown; native Canada, Greenland, Russian Federation, Svalbard and Jan Mayen; lower depth limit 1,500 m. Footer uses the assessment year 2017.
- Claim 1 (card 1): two teeth in the upper jaw; in males at 2–3 years the left tooth normally grows forward out through the front of the jaw; spiraled to the left (counter-clockwise), up to 9.8 ft (NOAA Fisheries). Tusk protrudes 2–3 m out of the upper left jaw, grows in a counterclockwise spiral (UW Laidre FAQ). Occasional tusked females / two-tusked males (both sources) — not stated on the poster.
- Claim 2 (card 2): in summer in High Arctic bays and fjords, males carefully cross their tusks, often with a female between them; scientific consensus is that the tusk is a sexual trait used to determine social rank (UW Laidre FAQ); NOAA: secondary sex characteristic for nonviolent assessment of hierarchical status. Copy says "thought to", not proven.
- Claim 3 (card 3): on offshore wintering grounds in dense pack ice, narwhals dive to at least 800 m 18–25 times per day for six months; can dive more than a mile when over 99% of the surface is ice (UW Laidre FAQ). NOAA: feed at or near the bottom in deep water; Greenland halibut-type flatfish ("turbot") in the diet → flatfish in figure 3.
- Main post (beyond cards): tusks exported from the Arctic reached Europe as early as the Middle Ages and became the source of the unicorn myth (UW Laidre FAQ).
- Story reply (beyond cards): calves born grey, turning mottled black and white with age, very old animals nearly white (NOAA; UW); returning from deep dives they look for a breathing crack in the ice using sound (UW).
- Not used: sensory-organ tusk claim (NOAA lists it only as possible; debated), 90-year lifespan (single method), population figures (80,000 vs 123,000 mature — differing sources), "corpse whale" etymology (would need a second source).

## Visual identity brief

- Individual/stage: one adult male (hero; tusk), two males plus one tuskless female (card 2), one adult diving (card 3).
- Biological identity references (saved in references/, Wikimedia Commons, downloaded at 1920 px via Special:FilePath with width in seconds):
  1. `Pod_Monodon_monoceros.jpg` — public domain (Kristin Laidre, UW/NOAA): lateral surface view of a tusked male; tusk exits the front of the upper jaw just above the mouth line, straight, tapering, spiral-grooved, ivory/yellowish; dense white-on-dark mottling; rounded head without beak.
  2. `Monodon_monoceros_pod.jpg` — public domain (Laidre, UW/NOAA): aerial dorsal view of a pod; no dorsal fin, short flippers, notched flukes, tusked and tuskless animals, older paler individuals.
  3. `Monodon_monoceros_cranio_e_dente_di_Narvalo_skull_and_tooth_of_Narwhal_museo_di_Bologna.JPG` — CC BY-SA 3.0 (Marco Vinci): skull with tusk rooted in the upper jaw (origin reference for card 1).
- Diagnostic invariants: (1) exactly one straight tapering spiral tusk emerging from the front of the upper jaw slightly left of the midline, not the forehead (refs 1, 3; NOAA); (2) no dorsal fin, only a low ridge (ref 2; NOAA); (3) small bulbous head, almost no beak, upturned mouthline (ref 1; NOAA); (4) dark grey-brown back densely mottled white, paler belly (refs 1, 2; NOAA/UW); (5) short blunt flippers and broad notched flukes with tips curling up in older animals (ref 2; NOAA).
- Hidden-anatomy check for card 1 (tooth origin): external aperture at the front of the upper jaw just above the mouth; opening direction forward along the body axis; origin from the left upper tooth socket inside the jaw; bilateral count two teeth, one erupted and one embedded; camera angle a semi-transparent top view of the head only.
- Natural pose: horizontal lateral swim, left side toward the viewer so the tusk's left-of-centre origin reads naturally; far flipper may be hidden.
- False silhouettes: beluga (white, no tusk, flexible neck), dolphins (beak, dorsal fin), swordfish/sawfish (fish fins), unicorn imagery (horn from forehead).
