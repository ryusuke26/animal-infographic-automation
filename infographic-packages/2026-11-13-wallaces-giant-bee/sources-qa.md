- Familiarity and discovery: the "largest bee" headline is known to some readers; the less familiar supported aspect is that the female lives inside an active termite nest and uses her jaws to gather resin that seals termites out (NHM London).
- Contribution to variety: first Hymenoptera; terrestrial forest nest instead of two consecutive fishes; black-and-amber engraved plate instead of watercolour.
- Conservation context: Global VU D2 (restricted range: three locations), 2013 assessment flagged "Needs updating". Copy states only the category; no population, trade or threat claim on the poster.
- Local-knowledge caution: copy never says "unknown" or "lost to everyone"; the story says scientific records and photographs, and the 1981 and 2019 finds involved local guides (NHM London).
- Selection rationale: strongest supported, highly visual discovery with direct official status and good specimen references, while breaking the fish run.

## Evidence Lock

- Exact taxon and names: *Megachile pluto* Smith, 1860 (subgenus *Callomegachile*; older combination *Chalicodoma pluto*). English: Wallace's Giant Bee. Japanese: ウォレスズ・ジャイアント・ビー (Japanese Wikipedia article title and Wikidata label; variant ウォレス・ジャイアント・ビー); not a standardized national-list name.
- Lineage: Animalia > Arthropoda > Insecta > Hymenoptera > Megachilidae > *Megachile* > *M. pluto*.
- IUCN check: official record read directly at https://www.iucnredlist.org/species/4410/21426160 on 2026-11-13: Global Vulnerable D2, ver 3.1, Date Assessed 11 December 2013, Year Published 2014, citation Kuhlmann, M. 2014, e.T4410A21426160, annotation "Needs updating", 3 locations, Indonesia. Footer uses the assessment year 2013. Mongabay (2019) wrongly calls it Data Deficient; not used for status.
- Range and setting: North Moluccas — Bacan, Halmahera, Tidore (NHM London); primary lowland forest (IUCN).
- Claim 1 (card 1): nests are built in mounds of tree-dwelling *Microcerotermes* termites, with tunnels and chambers dug into them; resin lines the inside, hardens and keeps termites out (NHM London; Messer 1984 as summarized by Dollin/Aussie Bee).
- Claim 2 (card 2): females use their large mandibles to collect and carry resin (NHM London); the resin is scraped into a ball and carried in the jaws (Messer's observations via Aussie Bee). "クワガタのような" follows Wallace's own description quoted by NHM ("immense jaws like a stag-beetle").
- Claim 3 (card 3): about four times the size of a honeybee; males much smaller and lack the large mandibles (NHM London). No cm values on the poster.
- Main post: "about the size of a human thumb" (NHM London) — context not on the cards.
- Story reply (beyond cards): Wallace collected only one female on Bacan; the jaws' purpose was unknown until Messer's 1981 observations on Halmahera; January 2019 team found a female after five days and it was photographed and filmed alive for the first time (NHM London; National Geographic 2019).

## Visual identity brief

- Individual/stage: adult female (the sex-specific jaws are the identity). Card 3 honeybee is a size cue only; no male is drawn because male appearance beyond "smaller, no large jaws" was not settled from references.
- Biological identity references (saved in references/):
  1. `Megachile_pluto_LACM_ENT_463926.jpeg` — LACM female, dorsal habitus with scale (CC BY 4.0, Natural History Museum of Los Angeles County): jaws, wing colour, cream band.
  2. `Megachile_pluto_10.5852-ejt.2025.1028.3129_Figure_55.png` — holotype female, Wood et al. (2025), European Journal of Taxonomy 1028, Fig. 55 (CC BY 4.0): B habitus, C head frontal (jaw shape, forked tips, eyes), D metasoma (cream band position).
  3. `Megachile_pluto_lateral_Naturalis.jpg` — Naturalis RMNH.INS.108876, lateral view (CC BY-SA 4.0): side profile, pale facial hair, folded amber wings.
- Diagnostic invariants: (1) two huge black forward-projecting sickle-shaped mandibles curving inward with small forked tips, roughly head-width long (ref 2C, ref 1); (2) broad glossy black head with large dark-brown oval eyes at the sides and pale buff hair on the lower face (ref 2C, ref 3); (3) black body with a single pale cream-buff hair band across the front of the abdomen only, rest black and segmented (ref 2D, ref 1); (4) two pairs of smoky amber/orange-brown translucent wings with dark veins (ref 1, ref 3); (5) robust bee body without a wasp waist, six black hairy legs, elbowed antennae.
- Natural pose: three-quarter front view from above standing on the nest surface; far-side legs may be partly hidden by the body.
- False silhouettes: stag beetle (hard elytra, no membranous wings shown at rest); carpenter bee *Xylocopa* (no huge jaws, no cream band); hornet/wasp (yellow stripes, thin waist).

## Copy Lock and art direction

Copy format `cards-v2`; nine strings in infographic-copy-ja.md / -en.md and verbatim prompt blocks. Main post adds thumb-scale context; story continues to the discovery history beyond the cards.

Style/medium: a hand-coloured 19th-century copperplate engraving plate, chosen because fine cross-hatching renders the glossy black body, hair band and sickle jaws precisely, the amber tint makes wings and resin glow, and the medium echoes the era of Wallace's 1858 collection.
