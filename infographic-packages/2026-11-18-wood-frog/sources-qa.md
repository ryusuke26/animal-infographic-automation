- Familiarity and discovery: the wood frog is common and familiar in North America, and its freeze tolerance is well known to biologists; the less familiar aspect for a general audience is that its heart and breathing stop while frozen and that the liver floods the body with glucose.
- Contribution to variety: first frog since 2026-09-17; first North American boreal/temperate forest floor in the latest eight; a winter-survival story rather than an anatomy or feeding hook; cut-paper collage medium after lithograph, gouache and engraving.
- Conservation context: Global LC (assessed 2020), population trend stable. Poster states only the category; no urgency.
- Local-knowledge caution: copy uses viewer-relative discovery and makes no "unknown" claim; the species is widely known in Canada and the USA.
- Selection rationale: a strong, explainable discovery with a directly confirmed official assessment date, filling the missing Amphibians group in a new region and story type.

## Evidence Lock

- Exact taxon and names: *Lithobates sylvaticus* (LeConte, 1825) as used by IUCN; synonym *Rana sylvatica* (widely used). English: Wood Frog. Japanese: アメリカアカガエル (used in Japanese science news coverage of the freeze-tolerance research, e.g. karapaia, nazology).
- Lineage: Animalia > Chordata > Amphibia > Anura > Ranidae > *Lithobates* (IUCN taxonomy).
- IUCN check: official record https://www.iucnredlist.org/species/58728/193382501 read directly 2026-11-18 — Global Least Concern, ver 3.1, Date Assessed 21 December 2020, Year Published 2022, citation IUCN SSC Amphibian Specialist Group 2022, e.T58728A193382501; trend Stable; Canada and USA. The older 2015 page (T58728A78907321) is marked "not the latest assessment". Footer uses the assessment year 2020.
- Claim 1 (card 1): burrows into decaying leaves before winter; freezes solid; "Throughout the entire winter, hibernating frogs are inanimate: they don't breathe and their hearts don't beat" (NPS Denali). ADW: breathing, blood flow and heartbeat stop during freezing.
- Claim 2 (card 2): at the first sign of freezing the liver converts stored glycogen to glucose, which circulates into the cells and lowers the freezing point of water (NPS Denali); glucose prevents intracellular freezing (ADW).
- Claim 3 (card 3): thaw and seek ponds for breeding; breeds in seasonal ponds in forest (NPS); among the first frogs to call, often before the ice is completely off the breeding ponds; call like a quacking duck (ADW).
- Main post (beyond cards): "found farther north than any other North American reptile or amphibian" (ADW).
- Story reply (beyond cards): Alaska field study (Larson et al. 2014, J Exp Biol 217:2193, via ScienceDaily 2014-01-06): hibernaculum temperatures -9 to -18 °C, all tracked frogs survived; glucose rose 2–10 times. "Robber's mask" nickname (ADW).
- Not used: percentage of body water frozen (sources give 35–45%, ~60%, 65–70% — conflicting), number of months frozen, NPS -12 °C figure (superseded in the story by the field study), organ-preservation applications.
